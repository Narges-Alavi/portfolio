Saveh Concert

A mobile app for concerts and cultural events in Saveh — most users will buy their first digital ticket here.

Constraint —International ticketing apps read cold and corporate. This product is cultural — Saveh has its own musical and event traditions — and had to feel warm without turning kitschy.
01 · Context & Problem

Discovery, artist and venue profiles, and the full ticket-purchase flow. The audience skews toward older users buying digital event tickets for the first time — many have only ever used paper tickets before.

02 · Research & Discovery
Conversations with older event-goers who’d only bought paper tickets before
Nobody asked me about payment security or seat-picker logic. They asked what the venue looked like, whether the singer was any good, and whether they could bring family. The app needed to answer those questions first, and treat the transaction second.
03 · Process
01
Home structure locked before any visual pass
The wireframe already had the carousel, then two grouped grids — a city-wide list, then a second one scoped to Vahdat Hall — split by a single promo banner. The floating three-icon nav (search, offers, home) was drawn this early too, and it's the one element that stays pinned through every other screen in the app, checkout included.
02
Capacity and full credits, not decoration
Both the wireframe and the final event page show remaining capacity per session — "29 spots left" next to one, "sold out" next to the other — and the complete crew list down to individual musicians, not just the headliner. That was a structural call made before any type or color existed: answer "is this worth going to" before the buyer reaches a price.
03
Price filter, simplified
The seat-map wireframe had a two-handle range slider — a minimum and a maximum price. It didn't survive to the final build; it became a single slider with one value. Two handles on a screen that narrow made the seat grid itself the thing fighting for thumb space.
04
Barcode became a QR code
The wireframe's ticket used a plain barcode placeholder. The final ticket scans as a QR code instead — closer to how door staff actually check people in. The 4.4% online-booking fee was already its own line item at the wireframe stage, before the visual design existed to hide it in.

05 · Solution

An event page that reads like a program note before it reads like a form, a two-state seat map that shows the exact price of a seat on tap, and a final ticket designed to look like an actual paper stub, perforated edge included.

06 · Impact & Results
Session capacity ("29 spots left" / "sold out") visible before anyone reaches the seat map
QR-based ticket instead of a barcode, matching how doors actually scan people in
One nav bar — search, offers, home — carried across every screen, checkout included
07 · Reflection
The two-handle price filter looked reasonable on paper. On an actual seat-map screen, a single slider turned out to be easier to operate with one thumb — the seat grid needed the space more than the second handle did.
Showing the 4.4% booking fee as its own line from the first wireframe is a call I'd make again. Folding it into the total would have read as a hidden fee the moment someone reached checkout.
Sold-out sessions still take up a full row in the session list. Next pass, I'd test whether that row should collapse instead of just getting grayed out.
