PathoFinder

AI-assisted radiographic measurement platform that turns hip, leg, spine and hand X-rays into clinical-grade morphology, alignment and bone-age reports — in daily use at hospitals and radiology centers in Iran.

Role
Sole Product Designer
Team
Solo — working directly with ML engineers and practicing radiologists
Timeframe
2023 — Present
Industry
Medical AI / Healthtech
Tools
Figma · FigJam
Platform
Web (Clinical Desktop)
Constraint —Every AI-generated measurement can shape a real clinical decision. Nothing is allowed to become final silently — a radiologist has to explicitly confirm each landmark before a study counts as reviewed.
01 · Context & Problem

PathoFinder reads a plain radiograph and returns the exact measurements a radiologist would otherwise calculate by hand — Tönnis angle, LCEA and CCD angle on a hip film, CPAK zone and full-leg alignment on a scanogram, curvature angles on a spine series, and a Greulich–Pyle bone-age stage on a hand X-ray. When auto-detection lands, the radiologist reviews and confirms it. When it fails — which happens often enough on real, noisy clinical films — the radiologist has to manually place the anatomical landmarks themselves before the study can move forward. My scope was that entire review layer: the four per-modality measurement workspaces, the manual-correction flow after a failed detection, the printable clinical report, and the study-list operations view radiologists and hospital staff use to triage hundreds of cases.

02 · Research & Discovery
Reviewed real failed-detection cases with radiologists at Erfan Niayesh Hospital · Mapped each clinical protocol (Tönnis, CCD, CPAK, Greulich–Pyle) to its own measurement vocabulary before touching a single screen
The hard problem was never "make the AI more accurate." It was that four completely different diagnostic protocols had to live inside one product without radiologists having to relearn the interface every time they switched from a hip film to a hand film.
03 · Process
01
The failure state came before the happy path
I designed the "auto-detection failed" recovery flow first, not the successful case. It surfaces as an ordered checklist of the exact landmarks still needed — femoral_head_center, femoral_head_top, greater_trochanter, medial_condyle_of_femur — each tappable directly onto the film, with the alignment numbers updating live as each point lands.
02
One grid, four clinical vocabularies
Hip morphology alone surfaces 12 distinct measurements. Rather than a bespoke layout per modality, I built one numbered-category grid — badge, clinical name, plain-language subtitle, value — and reused it for hip, leg alignment, spine and bone age. Radiologists learn the pattern once; only the content changes.
03
The report answers to someone who never opens the tool
The dense review workspace and the printed clinical report needed to share one data model but look nothing alike. I split them deliberately: a technical, editable workspace for the radiologist, and a plain letterhead-and-table report any partner hospital can print under its own branding for the referring physician.
04 · Solution

Four measurement workspaces sharing one interaction pattern: the film with landmark overlays in the center, a numbered category grid for that modality's clinical values, and — whenever auto-detection fails — a clear recovery flow that hands control back to the radiologist. Every workspace collapses into the same printable clinical report format, and a study only becomes Confirmed once a radiologist has explicitly signed off on it.

05 · Impact & Results
In active clinical use at Erfan Niayesh Hospital and partner radiology centers in Iran
+300 patients evaluated to date, with clinical decisions supported by measurements generated through the platform
Four diagnostic modules — hip morphology, leg alignment (CPAK), spinal curvature, bone age — shipped under one shared design system
The confirm-before-final pattern was adopted as the standard review step across every module, not just the one it launched with
06 · Reflection
The failure state, not the happy path, turned out to be the real design surface. A radiologist has to be willing to put their name on a number the model produced — that changes what "good UX" even means in a clinical tool.
Reusing one numbered-category grid across four unrelated clinical protocols kept the product learnable, but it meant resisting the temptation to redesign the layout every time a new measurement type shipped.
Working alone, directly with radiologists and ML engineers with no PM between us, taught me to bring a specific mock into every review, not an open question — a blank page went nowhere with clinicians, a concrete draft they could correct always did.
