01 · Context & Problem

PathoFinder is a B2B platform for hospitals and radiology centers. An AI model places landmarks on X-ray images, and the platform uses them to calculate the measurements radiologists would otherwise do by hand: hip angles, leg alignment and CPAK zone, spine curvature, and bone age. When the AI misses or misplaces a landmark, which happens on noisy real-world films, the radiologist marks where it should be, and its number is filled in. At the client's request, I collected the requirements of radiologists and doctors and designed around them: a measurement screen for each type of X-ray, the flow for fixing landmarks, the printable report, and the study list hospital staff work from.

02 · Research & Discovery
Interviews and feedback from doctors and radiology centers · Mapped each clinical protocol (Tönnis, CCD, CPAK, Greulich–Pyle) to its own measurement vocabulary before touching a single screen
The problem was never making the AI more accurate. There were two others. Four different diagnostic protocols had to live in one product, without radiologists having to relearn the interface every time they moved from a hip film to a hand film. And when the AI placed a landmark in the wrong spot, or missed it on a noisy X-ray, the radiologist needed a way to fix it and keep working instead of starting over.

I wrote each piece of feedback from doctors and radiology centers on its own sticky note, then grouped the notes by what caused the problem.

I can't verify this number

Won't act on a measurement they can't check or correct
Wants to see which landmarks the number came from
A wrong landmark should be fixable on the X-ray itself

The AI missed a landmark

A missed landmark must not block the whole study
Needs to know exactly which landmarks are still missing
After marking the missing landmark, its number should fill in by itself

Hard to read during a long day

Bright screens tire the eyes in dark reading rooms
Right-side and left-side numbers must be read quickly and without mistakes
Numbers should stand out from the text around them

The report has to travel

Referring doctors need a clean printout, not a screenshot of the workspace
Hospitals want their own letterhead on it
03 · Information Architecture

Radiologists move between hip, leg, spine and hand films all day, so the structure couldn't change every time the anatomy did. The product has three top-level areas: a study list to triage from, one workspace per anatomy, and a printable report. Everything inside a workspace follows the same layout.

04 · User Flow

The main flow follows one study from arrival to sign-off. The branch I spent the most time on is the one where the AI misses or misplaces a landmark: it isn't an error screen, it's a second route through the same product that ends at the same review and the same confirmation.

05 · Process
01
The failure state came before the happy path
I designed the "auto-detection failed" recovery flow first, not the successful case. It surfaces as an ordered checklist of the exact landmarks still needed — femoral_head_center, femoral_head_top, greater_trochanter, medial_condyle_of_femur. The radiologist only marks on the film where each missing landmark is, and its number is filled in.
02
One grid, four clinical vocabularies
Hip alone surfaces 12 distinct measurements. Rather than a bespoke layout per modality, I built one numbered-category grid — badge, clinical name, plain-language subtitle, value — and reused it for hip, leg alignment, spine and bone age. Radiologists learn the pattern once; only the content changes.
03
The report answers to someone who never opens the tool
The dense review workspace and the printed clinical report needed to share one data model but look nothing alike. I split them deliberately: a technical, editable workspace for the radiologist, and a plain letterhead-and-table report any partner hospital can print under its own branding for the referring physician.
06 · Solution

Four measurement workspaces sharing one interaction pattern: the film with landmark overlays in the center, a numbered category grid for that modality's clinical values, and — whenever auto-detection fails — a clear recovery flow that hands control back to the radiologist. Every workspace collapses into the same printable clinical report format, and a study only becomes Confirmed once a radiologist has explicitly signed off on it.

08 · Impact & Results
In active clinical use at Erfan Niayesh Hospital and partner radiology centers in Iran
+300 patients evaluated to date, with clinical decisions supported by measurements generated through the platform
Four diagnostic modules — hip, leg alignment (CPAK), spinal curvature, bone age — shipped under one shared design system
The confirm-before-final pattern was adopted as the standard review step across every module, not just the one it launched with
09 · Reflection
The failure state, not the happy path, turned out to be the real design surface. A radiologist has to be willing to put their name on a number the model produced — that changes what "good UX" even means in a clinical tool.
Reusing one numbered-category grid across four unrelated clinical protocols kept the product learnable, but it meant resisting the temptation to redesign the layout every time a new measurement type shipped.
Working alone, directly with radiologists and ML engineers with no PM between us, taught me to bring a specific mock into every review, not an open question — a blank page went nowhere with clinicians, a concrete draft they could correct always did.
