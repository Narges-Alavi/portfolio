FASHION E-COMMERCE · CHECKOUT FLOW

Samin Style (سمین استایل)

A practice project covering a whole fashion storefront — homepage through a three-step checkout. The interesting problem turned out not to be the product pages but the seams between them, where a buyer gets stuck mid-purchase.

Role
Self-directed practice project — sole designer
Team
Solo
Scope
11 desktop screens — home, category, listing & filters, product page, cart, address, payment, about, contact
Industry
Fashion & Apparel / E-Commerce
Platform
Desktop (Web) · RTL Persian
Tools
Figma
Constraint —A practice project — no client, no users, no analytics. I set the scope myself, and I made it wide on purpose: enough screens that they have to work as a flow rather than as eleven separate compositions.
01 · Context & Problem

A fashion storefront is easy to design one screen at a time and easy to break as a sequence. The individual pages hold up fine in isolation — a homepage, a product grid, a product page. The failures happen in the seams: a buyer picks a size that is not stocked, reaches a payment screen without an address, or taps a "continue" button that does nothing and does not say why.

So I built the full path rather than a few hero screens, specifically so those seams were forced into view instead of staying hypothetical.

02 · Research & Discovery
No user research — a review of checkout and listing patterns across Persian fashion stores and general marketplaces
Listing and product pages were largely a solved problem: filters, swatches and size chips were conventional almost everywhere. Checkout was where the pages actually diverged. Some let you reach payment with an incomplete address and only failed at the end; others disabled the next step correctly but never said which field was missing. The pattern worth designing around was not "make checkout shorter" — it was "never let the buyer advance into an error."
03 · Process
01
A three-step stepper, visible on all three steps
Cart → address and delivery → payment, with the stepper pinned to the top of each. The remaining work is visible from the cart, so the commitment is known up front rather than revealed one screen at a time.
02
Blocking the step instead of failing at the end
On the address step the continue button stays disabled while a banner names the problem — required fields are incomplete. The payment step does the same for an unselected payment method. The buyer gets stopped where the fix is, not after submitting.
03
Stock limits stated at the point of the mistake
Raising a quantity past what is stocked shows the ceiling inline, next to that line item, so the wrong number and the message about it occupy the same place. The cart also warns that adding an item is not a reservation.
04
An order summary that persists across the whole checkout
Item count, cart subtotal, saving and total sit in a fixed panel on every step, along with the coupon field — so the price never disappears at the moment the buyer is deciding whether to keep going.
05
Filters as toggles plus facets, resolved in place
The listing page pairs three quick toggles — in stock, newest, most popular — with checkbox facets for garment type and style and a two-handle price range. Filtering does not navigate away, so comparing the filtered result against the unfiltered one is the same screen.
06
Variants that communicate availability, not just options
Colour swatches and size chips on the product page show which combinations exist, so an unavailable size reads as unavailable rather than failing after "add to cart".
07
One trust row, deliberately repeated
Fast delivery, 14-day returns, authenticity — the same three-badge row appears at the foot of the listing, product and cart pages, so the reassurance is present at each hesitation point instead of only on the homepage.

05 · Impact & Results

No metrics — nothing here was built or used, so there is nothing to measure. What the flow does structurally:

Each checkout step is reachable only when its own requirements are met, and a blocked step names the requirement that is missing.
Quantity and stock conflicts surface in the cart, beside the affected line, before the buyer ever reaches payment.
The order total and coupon field stay visible on all three checkout steps without scrolling.
The listing page resolves filtered and unfiltered states on the same screen, so nothing about the result set has to be held in memory.
06 · Reflection
Designing the whole path rather than a few hero screens changed what I noticed. Almost every decision I am happy with here is about a transition between screens, not the composition of one.
Every error and empty state in this project is one I imagined. A real store would have a list of the ones that actually happen, and it would not match mine.
The homepage is the weakest screen. It is long and stacks a lot of merchandising bands, and I never established which of them earn their place — that is a question about the business, and I did not have one to ask.
No mobile designs. For an apparel store, where a large share of browsing happens on a phone, that is the biggest hole in the project rather than a nice-to-have.
The product photography is placeholder imagery from other brands, and it quietly does a lot of the visual work here. A real version would have to hold up with the client's own shots.
